\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This project reimplemented a published KAN-ODE recipe from its components
(a KAN layer with seven interchangeable bases, six explicit Runge-Kutta
integrators and an instrumented training loop), verified them against known
solutions, and reproduced the recipe on the Lotka-Volterra predator-prey
system. It extended the recipe to two additional dynamical systems (a
damped pendulum and an SIR epidemic model), diagnosed and fixed two
genuine cross-domain training failures before running five further studies
that each isolated a specific question about solver behavior, gradient
dynamics, basis-function design, damping (originally intended as a
stiffness probe), and comparison against a fundamentally different modeling
paradigm.

The principal quantitative findings are:

\begin{itemize}
\item At 10{,}000 epochs, KAN-ODE extrapolation error on Lotka-Volterra is
  essentially flat out to twice the original horizon ($1.1\times$
  degradation), while a parameter-matched MLP degrades $8.3\times$ -- a
  $40.6\times$ KAN-ODE advantage (Table~\ref{tab:extrap-horizon}). At
  50{,}000 epochs, with gradient clipping and on a different platform
  (Section~\ref{sec:phase4}), this
  reverses, and the MLP is $6.8$--$10.8\times$ more accurate at every horizon
  (Table~\ref{tab:extrap-horizon-50k}). The KAN-ODE reaches a
  well-extrapolating field sooner; it does not reach a better one on this
  problem.
\item Of the base paper's Lotka-Volterra claims, the 240-parameter model's
  accuracy is reproduced and its per-epoch cost relative to an MLP closely
  matched ($2.3\times$ against the paper's $2.7\times$); the
  tenfold epoch advantage is not (the MLP reaches $10^{-3}$ loss $4.1\times$
  sooner; in the clipped 50{,}000-epoch reruns the KAN-ODE reaches $10^{-3}$
  first but $10^{-4}$ later, neither by a factor near ten), and the generalization advantage holds only at the shorter budget
  (Table~\ref{tab:paper-claims}).
\item SIR's training failure traced to a roughly $18$--$19\times$ unit-scale
  mismatch between the network's initial field and the true dynamics. A
  change of time units and a conservation projection, which assume nothing
  beyond SIR's own equations, remove the divergence and the mass drift but
  leave extrapolation $R^2$ at $-0.145$ (from $-20.97$); adding a
  vanishing-field gate on the infected fraction, a physical prior, raises it
  to $+0.9981$.
\item The implementation is verified independently of the benchmarks: every
  integrator reaches its theoretical order of convergence
  (Table~\ref{tab:observed-order}), and 249 of 250 automated tests pass;
  the remaining one, which needs a GPU, is skipped.
\item Direct autograd is the correct default for this project's scale:
  adjoint sensitivity already uses less memory at every tested trajectory
  length but costs a consistent $1.9$--$2.1\times$ wall-clock overhead.
\item Gradient-norm roughness does not explain Forward Euler's convergence
  floor (Spearman $\rho=-0.088$, $p=0.87$); the more likely mechanism is a
  systematic bias in the learned field, consistent with the solver ablation's
  own Lipschitz-constant measurements.
\item A learnable blend of B-spline and RBF basis functions never outperforms
  the better pure basis on Lotka-Volterra, or pure RBF on the pendulum (no
  pure B-spline pendulum run was made), and measuring each basis's
  actual contribution shows the learned gate is a fair guide to how much each
  is used.
\item The damping sweep never reached a stiff regime, and no cell diverged,
  as expected from the small step size ($|h\lambda|\le0.30$), which keeps
  every solver's discrete map close to the learned field's own flow (the only
  predicted instability is a mild anti-damping for Euler at $\mu=0.1$). Its
  failures at $\mu\in\{0.5,1.0,2.0\}$ trace to random initialization rather
  than solver order, apart from one Euler-specific failure at $\mu=1.0$,
  confirmed at $\mu=2.0$ by a genuine, attracting spurious equilibrium that
  Newton's method locates in all four solvers' learned fields.
\item With finite-difference derivatives, sparse regression is less
  accurate than KAN-ODE at every noise level but degrades far less ($9\times$
  vs.\ $1540\times$ from clean to $\sigma=0.1$, starting from a much higher
  noise-free error). With exact
  derivatives it recovers the true equation, so on clean data the ranking
  reflects the derivative estimator, not the method. Two of the SIR
  stability fixes transfer to a synthetic, non-mechanistic epidemic curve;
  the one that transfers best was predicted inapplicable.
\end{itemize}

Every quantitative claim in this report traces to a saved metrics file, a
regenerable figure, or a stated post-hoc analysis of a saved checkpoint, and
every study that tested a specific mechanistic hypothesis reports the outcome
of a direct control arm rather than the hypothesis alone.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

The conclusions above are bounded by the following, each stated where it
applies and collected here.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Single seed.} Almost every result rests on one random seed.
  The damping study, where extending from one seed to three dissolved a
  headline finding, and the $3.5\times$ gap between the two second-order
  solvers (Table~\ref{tab:solver-ablation}), show how large single-seed
  variance can be.
\item \textbf{Unmeasured recipe differences.} The audit
  (Table~\ref{tab:paper-audit}) found three differences from the paper's code
  whose effect has not been measured: the learning rate, the initial-weight
  scale and the RBF width. None was tested at the paper's values (the
  learning rate was varied only downward, to $5\times10^{-4}$). Retraining the paper-specification MLP with the
  paper's learning rate and initialization is the single most informative
  next run, because it would show whether that MLP's failure here comes from
  the recipe or from the architecture.
\item \textbf{Budget, clipping and platform.} The KAN-versus-MLP reversal at
  50{,}000 epochs coincides with the introduction of gradient clipping and a
  change of platform (Windows with PyTorch 2.5.1 for the original runs, 2.13
  for the SiLU MLP; Linux with PyTorch 2.10 for the reruns), and neither was separated from the
  budget. Only five configurations were retrained at the longer
  budget.
\item \textbf{Scope of the ablations.} The solver, basis and step-size
  ablations, the budget check and the sparse-regression comparison were run
  on Lotka-Volterra only.
\item \textbf{No stiff system, no implicit solver.} The damping sweep's
  largest timescale ratio is $4.3$, and every solver is explicit, so this
  report says nothing about stiffness proper.
\item \textbf{Synthetic data only.} Every dataset, including the
  non-mechanistic epidemic curve, is generated rather than recorded; no real
  observational data was used.
\item \textbf{Derivative estimation in the baseline.} Sparse regression was
  fitted to finite-difference derivatives, which dominate its noise-free
  error (Table~\ref{tab:sindy-derivative}); a weak or trajectory-fitted
  formulation was not tried.
\item \textbf{Hardware.} All timings are CPU timings on two different
  machines, except the GPU memory study, and are comparable only within a
  machine.
\end{itemize}
