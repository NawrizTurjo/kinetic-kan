\section{Sparse Regression Comparison and Non-Mechanistic Epidemic Validation}
\label{sec:track-e}

This section compares two fundamentally different ways of discovering
governing equations from data -- sparse symbolic regression and gradient-based
neural fitting -- and separately tests whether the cross-domain stability
fixes of Section~\ref{sec:phase2} generalize beyond the mechanistic system
that produced them.

\subsection{Noise robustness: KAN-ODE vs.\ sparse symbolic regression}

Sparse Identification of Nonlinear Dynamics (SINDy) \cite{brunton2016sindy}
fits a governing equation as a sparse linear combination of candidate terms
drawn from a fixed library -- here, every polynomial in $x$ and $y$ up to
degree 2, i.e.\ $\{1,x,y,x^2,xy,y^2\}$. Coefficients are estimated by
sequentially thresholded least squares: an ordinary least-squares fit is
performed against numerically estimated derivatives, every coefficient
below a fixed threshold is zeroed out, and the fit is repeated on the
surviving terms until the active set stops changing. This is what makes the
recovered model sparse and, in principle, human-readable -- unlike the KAN's
240 free parameters, SINDy returns a short list of surviving terms with
numeric coefficients attached, directly comparable to the true equation's
own coefficients. The implementation is \texttt{pysindy}'s
\cite{desilva2020pysindy,kaptanoglu2022pysindy} sequentially
thresholded least squares with threshold $0.05$, held fixed across the
entire noise sweep so that the comparison measures noise sensitivity rather
than per-level tuning. The derivatives it regresses against are estimated
by plain finite differences from the observations. It is fit on the same 36
training observations the KAN-ODE noise-sweep models of
Table~\ref{tab:noise} were trained on
($t\in[0,3.5]$, $\Delta t=0.1$, noise $\sigma\in\{0,0.01,0.05,0.1\}$ added to
the training points; Section~\ref{sec:setup}). The recovered equation is then
integrated over $[0,14]$ and scored against the clean ground truth, exactly
as the KAN-ODE is.

The two methods are not fitted the same way. KAN-ODE is trained through the
integrator on trajectory error, while SINDy fits pointwise derivative
estimates, so SINDy's accuracy is limited by how well 36 points at spacing
$0.1$ determine a derivative. That limit turns out to dominate the
comparison, as the check at the end of this subsection shows.

\begin{expectbox}
\expectrow{Question}{How does a sparse, symbolic method compare to a dense, 240-parameter neural method as observation noise increases?}
\expectrow{Expected}{Sparse regression's tiny library should make it more robust to noise; KAN-ODE's larger parameter count should make it more accurate.}
\expectrow{Observed}{With finite-difference derivatives, both hold: KAN-ODE is more accurate at every noise level, but its advantage collapses from $1110\times$ to $6.5\times$. Given exact derivatives, SINDy recovers the true equation outright, so the accuracy ranking reflects the derivative estimator, not the two model classes.}
\end{expectbox}

parameters fit the noise directly and the error compounds through the
solver.

\paragraph{How much of SINDy's error is the derivative estimate?} SINDy's
noise-free error of $9.9\times10^{-2}$ is surprisingly large for a library
that contains the exact Lotka-Volterra terms. To separate the method from
its derivative estimator, the noise-free fit was repeated with the exact
derivatives, computed from Equation~\ref{eq:lv} at the same 36 points, in
place of the finite differences (Table~\ref{tab:sindy-derivative}).

With exact derivatives SINDy recovers the true equation to three decimals
and extrapolates eight orders of magnitude better than KAN-ODE. The finite
differences carry only a $3.5\%$ root-mean-square error in the derivatives,
but that is enough for the fit to admit two spurious terms, a constant and a
linear $x$ term, which then accumulate over the extrapolation window. On
noise-free data, therefore, the ranking in Table~\ref{tab:sindy-noise} is a
statement about finite-difference derivatives at this sampling density, not
about sparse regression against KAN-ODE. A fairer comparison would fit SINDy
without pointwise derivatives, for example with a weak (integral) formulation
or by fitting its coefficients to trajectory error as the KAN-ODE is. Neither
was run, and whether SINDy would keep its advantage under noise is untested.
Table~\ref{tab:sindy-terms} lists the coefficient each fit recovers for the
four true terms. The bilinear cross-term ($xy$) is recovered to within
$5.7\%$ at every noise level. What degrades with noise is the linear
coefficient $\alpha$ of $x$, from $2.0\%$ to $44.6\%$ error, together with the
number of spurious terms admitted by the fixed threshold, which rises from 2
to 7--8. SINDy's breakdown at $\sigma\ge0.05$ is therefore a failure to pin the
linear rates, not a failure to find the interaction structure.

\paragraph{Smoothed derivatives do not help here.} \texttt{pysindy}'s smoothed
finite differences, a common defence against noisy derivatives, were also
tried. They recover the coefficients worse than plain finite differences at
every noise level: the worst term's error is $87\%$ at $\sigma=0$ and $101\%$ at
$\sigma=0.1$, against $5.4\%$ and $44.6\%$, and the fit keeps 8 spurious terms at
every level. In extrapolation they are worse at three of the four noise
levels (only at $\sigma=0.01$ are they slightly better, $0.133$ against
$0.158$). The cause is the filter window rather than the method: the default
Savitzky-Golay window of 11 points spans $1.1$ time units at $\Delta t=0.1$, a
third of the $3.32$-unit cycle and nearly a third of the whole 36-point
training series, so the smoothing flattens the peaks the derivative
information lives in.

The cross-term result matters for the comparison because $xy$ is exactly the
term a single-layer KAN
edge, being strictly univariate, cannot represent directly, since each edge
is a function of only one input variable and a layer sums its edges
additively. This asymmetry -- sparse regression writes $xy$ as a literal
library column, while a KAN can only approximate it through composition
across a hidden layer -- means a term-by-term symbolic comparison would be
comparing a coefficient against a distributed representation, so the
comparison used here is trajectory reconstruction instead
(Figure~\ref{fig:sindy-trajectories}).

\subsection{Edge pruning: testing whether a term-by-term comparison is even possible}

Part of the original motivation for a smooth, globally-supported basis such
as RBF is that a sparse, pruned KAN edge structure could in principle be read
back as a closed-form symbolic law, the same interpretability goal sparse
regression achieves by construction. The base paper reaches this by adding an
$L_1$ penalty on every spline coefficient to the training loss,
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L} = \mathrm{MSE}(\mathbf{u}_{\text{true}}, \mathbf{u}_{\text{pred}}) + \lambda\sum_{i} |c_i|,
\label{eq:l1-sparsity}
\end{equation}
where $c_i$ ranges over every spline coefficient in Equation~\ref{eq:kan-edge}
and $\lambda$ trades off trajectory accuracy against how many edges the
penalty drives toward zero. Every checkpoint trained for this project used
$\lambda=0$: the plain mean-squared-error term alone, with no sparsity
pressure ever added to Equation~\ref{eq:l1-sparsity}. A KAN-ODE analogue of a
sparse coefficient list would instead come from pruning weak edges
\emph{post hoc} and reading off what survives. Ranking each edge by mean
spline-coefficient magnitude and masking the weakest, applied to the existing
(never retrained) noise-sweep checkpoints, removes the two weakest of forty
edges and raises the noise-free full-horizon MSE from $8.90\times10^{-5}$ to
$3.43$ -- a factor of $3.9\times10^{4}$, worse than predicting the constant
dataset mean ($2.91$; Figure~\ref{fig:pruning}). Every checkpoint was trained with no sparsity-inducing
regularization applied at all, so nothing during training ever asked the
network to concentrate its fit onto a few edges; the flat plateau at
$\approx3.5$--$3.8$ across all pruning levels of 25\% or more is the
signature of a model where the fit is spread across many edges: no small
subset of edges carries it, so there is no sparse structure for a
magnitude-based pruning rule to recover. This says nothing about whether the
pruning procedure would find such structure in a network trained to have
it; that would need the $L_1$-regularized retraining of
Equation~\ref{eq:l1-sparsity}, which was not run.

\subsection{Testing whether SIR's fixes transfer to a non-mechanistic epidemic curve}

A synthetic, asymmetric outbreak curve generated by no compartmental ODE
(Equation~\ref{eq:synthetic-epidemic}, Table~\ref{tab:datasets}), tests whether the SIR fixes of
Section~\ref{sec:phase2} generalize by mechanism rather than by coincidence
of the SIR system's own structure. The same init-time diagnostic used to
root-cause SIR reproduces an almost identical speed-mismatch signature
($18.77\times$ against SIR's $19\times$), which says that time rescaling is
needed. It does not by itself set the factor. The factor comes from a
horizon rule: choose $s$ so that the rescaled horizon is $T/s=5$, the regime in
which Lotka-Volterra ($T=3.5$) and the fixed pendulum ($T=5$) train. For SIR
this rule gives the $s=10$ already used ($50/10=5$). For the epidemic curve it
was applied to the full 120-day record, giving $s=120/5=24$; applied to the
45-day training window, as for SIR, it would give $s=9$. The time-scale sweep
(Figure~\ref{fig:time-scale-sweep}) was run in the same batch as the $s=24$
arms, so the project's records cannot show that 24 was fixed before the
sweep's results were seen. The sweep's training loss is, however, flat
across $s\in[10,40]$, so it could not have singled out 24, and every arm
below is compared at the same $s$.

\begin{expectbox}
\expectrow{Question}{SIR's three stability fixes were each derived from a property of the SIR generator itself -- do they still work on data no compartmental model produced?}
\expectrow{Expected}{Only the unit-rescaling fix, which assumes nothing about the data's structure, should transfer; the two structural fixes should not.}
\expectrow{Observed}{One in three predictions was wrong: the vanishing-field gate transfers and is the best single option, despite being predicted inapplicable.}
\end{expectbox}

Table~\ref{tab:fix-transfer} summarizes the outcome. The vanishing-field
gate was \emph{predicted} inapplicable: the infected
fraction at $t=0$ is $0.0027$, seemingly too small to avoid freezing the
field entirely. A control arm overturns that prediction: gating the learned
field by the initial state correctly encodes that the rate of change is
proportional to current prevalence, and is the only configuration that
reproduces the existence of a peak (day 48 predicted vs.\ true day 45) from a
training window that never observed one.

\paragraph{The extrapolation runaway traces to the train/test split.} The
default split lands exactly on the infected peak (0\% of the decay phase is
in the training window), so a model has to infer a decay it has never
observed. The rescaling-only model ($s=24$, no gate, 2{,}000 epochs) fails to:
its predicted infected curve never turns over and reaches $6.18\times$ the
true peak (extrapolation MSE $6.99$). The gate model above is the exception
that the structural prior makes possible. The split controls retrain the
same rescaling-only configuration with the cut moved later. Moving it just
15 days, to day 60, collapses the predicted maximum from $6.18$ to $0.964$
(true maximum $=1.0$), turning over at day 47 against a true day 45
(Figure~\ref{fig:real-epidemic-fit}, right). So the runaway \emph{peak} is a
property of where the window ends. The tail is not fixed: past day 70 that
same model's prediction drops steeply below zero, and its extrapolation MSE
is still $0.397$. Seeing the peak is enough to place it, but not enough to
learn how the decay ends.

\begin{keypoint}[Verdict]
Time rescaling transfers unconditionally because it is a unit change with no
structural precondition. The other two fixes are structural priors that
transfer exactly insofar as their structure is actually present in the new
data: the conservation projection's precondition is confirmable in advance
by directly measuring the compartment sum, while the vanishing-field gate's
precondition turned out to require running the control arm to settle --
argument alone predicted the wrong outcome. That arm cost 27 minutes and
overturned the prediction.
\end{keypoint}
