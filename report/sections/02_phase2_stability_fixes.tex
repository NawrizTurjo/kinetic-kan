\section{Benchmark Ablation and Cross-Domain Stability Analysis}
\label{sec:phase2}

Before extending KAN-ODE to new systems, two questions needed settling: which
solver and basis choices this architecture actually prefers, and whether the
baseline recipe (Section~\ref{sec:phase1}) transfers unchanged to systems
beyond Lotka-Volterra. Both were answered empirically, and the second
uncovered two genuine training failures that required a mechanistic diagnosis
before they could be fixed.

\subsection{Solver, basis and architecture ablation}

Every integrator in Table~\ref{tab:solver-ablation} advances the state by one
step $\Delta t$ using only evaluations of the learned field
$\mathbf{f}_\theta$, and all were implemented directly for this project
rather than called from an external ODE library. Writing $\mathbf{u}_n$ for
the state at step $n$:
\begin{align}
\text{Forward Euler (order 1):}\quad & \mathbf{u}_{n+1} = \mathbf{u}_n + \Delta t\, \mathbf{f}_\theta(\mathbf{u}_n) \label{eq:euler}\\
\text{Midpoint (order 2):}\quad & \mathbf{k}_1 = \mathbf{f}_\theta(\mathbf{u}_n), \quad
  \mathbf{u}_{n+1} = \mathbf{u}_n + \Delta t\,\mathbf{f}_\theta\!\left(\mathbf{u}_n + \tfrac{\Delta t}{2}\mathbf{k}_1\right) \label{eq:midpoint}\\
\text{Heun's method (order 2):}\quad & \mathbf{k}_1 = \mathbf{f}_\theta(\mathbf{u}_n), \quad
  \mathbf{k}_2 = \mathbf{f}_\theta(\mathbf{u}_n+\Delta t\,\mathbf{k}_1), \quad
  \mathbf{u}_{n+1} = \mathbf{u}_n + \tfrac{\Delta t}{2}(\mathbf{k}_1+\mathbf{k}_2) \label{eq:heun}\\
\text{Classical RK4 (order 4):}\quad & \mathbf{u}_{n+1} = \mathbf{u}_n + \tfrac{\Delta t}{6}\left(\mathbf{k}_1+2\mathbf{k}_2+2\mathbf{k}_3+\mathbf{k}_4\right) \label{eq:rk4}
\end{align}
with the classical RK4 stages $\mathbf{k}_1=\mathbf{f}_\theta(\mathbf{u}_n)$,
$\mathbf{k}_2=\mathbf{f}_\theta(\mathbf{u}_n+\tfrac{\Delta t}{2}\mathbf{k}_1)$,
$\mathbf{k}_3=\mathbf{f}_\theta(\mathbf{u}_n+\tfrac{\Delta t}{2}\mathbf{k}_2)$,
$\mathbf{k}_4=\mathbf{f}_\theta(\mathbf{u}_n+\Delta t\,\mathbf{k}_3)$. Here
$\Delta t$ stands for the integration step, which in every run is half the
data spacing (two substeps, Algorithm~\ref{alg:odeint}). DOPRI5 and Tsit5
follow the same stage-and-combine pattern at order 5 (Equation~\ref{eq:rk-general}),
each with a seven-stage Butcher tableau (Appendix~\ref{app:butcher}) whose
last stage has zero weight, so both cost six field evaluations per step at a
fixed step size. Their coefficients are chosen so the error terms of orders
one through four cancel exactly; the two differ in which remaining error
constant they minimize, and Tsit5's is about $5\times$ smaller on the test
problem of Table~\ref{tab:observed-order}. Every extra stage in Equations~\ref{eq:midpoint}--\ref{eq:rk4}
costs one additional evaluation of $\mathbf{f}_\theta$, which is why function
evaluations per epoch, not solver order alone, is the operative cost measure
in Table~\ref{tab:solver-ablation}.

Table~\ref{tab:solver-ablation} sweeps these six explicit Runge-Kutta integrators at
fixed radial-basis-function (RBF) basis; Table~\ref{tab:basis-ablation}
sweeps seven basis representations at fixed Tsit5 solver. Both use the
protocol of Section~\ref{sec:phase1}. The number of function evaluations per
training epoch (Equation~\ref{eq:nfe}) is reported because it is the dominant
cost driver for training time. The Lipschitz estimate $L$ of the trained
field (Equation~\ref{eq:lipschitz}) -- the largest rate at which the output
of $\mathbf{f}_\theta$ changes per unit change of its input, measured along
the training trajectory -- is reported alongside
accuracy because it is a direct measure of how smooth or jagged the basis
expansion in Equation~\ref{eq:kan-edge} ended up, independent of the
trajectory error.

Truncation error dominates only at order $p=1$: Euler's extrapolation MSE is
$28\times$ Tsit5's, and it is the only solver that fails to reach $10^{-4}$
training loss within budget. Its trained field is also the least smooth of
the six ($L=8.55$, the highest in the table), consistent with a systematic
bias forcing the network to fit a sharper correction than the smoother
integrators require. Midpoint, RK4, DOPRI5 and Tsit5 then collapse into a
$4.1\%$ spread in extrapolation error with no monotonic order dependence,
which suggests that at $h=0.05$ integrator truncation error has fallen below
the optimization noise floor. Heun is the exception: it has the same order
and the same cost as Midpoint, yet extrapolates $3.5\times$ worse
($3.10\times10^{-4}$ against $8.95\times10^{-5}$). Two methods of the same
order and cost should not differ systematically by that much, so this gap
most likely reflects single-seed variance in where training settles. It also
means the single-seed spread among second-order methods is at least
$3.5\times$, which limits how much any one solver ranking in this table can
be trusted. On this seed Midpoint matches Tsit5's accuracy within $0.3\%$ at
one third of the function evaluations, but given Heun's result, calling it the
cost-normalized optimum would need more seeds to confirm.

The wall-clock column confirms that function evaluations are the operative
cost. Training times of $254:499:498:1019:1699:1712$~s follow the evaluation
counts' ratios of $1:2:2:4:6:6$ almost exactly, because backpropagation
unrolls every stage of every step, so each extra stage adds forward and
backward work in proportion. This close match also indicates that the
timings are not contaminated by scheduling noise. Figure~\ref{fig:solver-curves}
shows the training-loss histories: the five higher-order solvers track each
other closely throughout, while Euler separates after about 2{,}000 epochs
and descends more slowly for the rest of the run.

\paragraph{Step size.} A separate sweep holds Tsit5 and the RBF basis fixed
and varies the data spacing $\Delta t\in\{0.05,0.1,0.2\}$ over the same
window $[0,3.5]$, so the integration step $h=\Delta t/2$ and the number of
training points change together (Table~\ref{tab:stepsize}). Tsit5's
truncation error at these step sizes is tiny: on the test problem of
Table~\ref{tab:observed-order} it is $\sim10^{-10}$ at $h=0.1$, while the
extrapolation errors here correspond to root-mean-square errors of
$\sim10^{-2}$. The differences between rows are therefore very unlikely to
come from the integrator's accuracy, and the data agree: the finest step is
\emph{not} the most accurate. Extrapolation
error is lowest at $\Delta t=0.1$, $1.5\times$ higher at $0.05$ and
$5.1\times$ higher at $0.2$, where only 19 training points remain. Halving
the step doubles the function evaluations and the wall-clock time without
buying accuracy. For a learned field, the step size matters through
training (how many points the loss sees and how deep the backpropagation
graph is), not through classical truncation error.
Figure~\ref{fig:stepsize-loglog} shows the same data on log-log axes next to
first- and second-order reference slopes: the extrapolation error follows
neither, and the training error is almost flat across the three step sizes.

Writing $z_g$ for the $g$-th of $G$ evenly spaced grid centers with spacing
$h$, and $u=\tanh(x)\in[-1,1]$ the normalized edge input, the seven
bases compared here (Figure~\ref{fig:basis-shapes}) are
\begin{equation}
\begin{gathered}
B_g^{\text{RBF}}(u)=\exp\!\left(-\tfrac12\left(\tfrac{u-z_g}{h}\right)^2\right),\\
B_g^{\text{spline}}(u) = \text{Cox--de Boor recursion of degree }k\text{ on the clamped knot vector of Section~\ref{sec:numerics}},
\end{gathered}
\label{eq:basis-rbf-spline}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
B_g^{\text{Cheb}}(u)=T_g(u)=\cos(g\arccos u), \qquad
B_g^{\text{Lag}}(u)=\prod_{j\neq g}\frac{u-z_j}{z_g-z_j}, \qquad
B_g^{\text{Newt}}(u)=\prod_{j<g}(u-z_j),
\label{eq:basis-poly}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
B_g^{\text{RSWAF}}(u)=\operatorname{sech}^2\!\left(\tfrac{u-z_g}{h}\right)=1-\tanh^2\!\left(\tfrac{u-z_g}{h}\right), \qquad
B_g^{\text{IQF}}(u)=\frac{1}{1+\left(\frac{u-z_g}{h}\right)^2},
\label{eq:basis-rational}
\end{equation}
each substituted for $B_i(x)$ in Equation~\ref{eq:kan-edge}. RSWAF
(reflectional switch activation function) and IQF (inverse quadratic) are,
like the Gaussian, bell-shaped kernels centred on each grid point. The
Gaussian and RSWAF decay exponentially away from the centre, while IQF decays
only as $1/r^2$ and so has much heavier tails. The Cox--de
Boor recursion builds a degree-$k$ B-spline from degree-0 indicator functions
by repeated linear blending over the knots $t_g$ (the clamped knot vector,
not the grid centres $z_g$), $B_{g,0}(u)=\mathbf{1}[t_g\le u<t_{g+1}]$ and
$B_{g,p}(u)=\frac{u-t_g}{t_{g+p}-t_g}B_{g,p-1}(u)+\frac{t_{g+p+1}-u}{t_{g+p+1}-t_{g+1}}B_{g+1,p-1}(u)$,
with a term dropped whenever its denominator vanishes at a repeated knot.
This is what gives it compact support: $B_{g,k}(u)=0$ once $u$ leaves the
$k+2$ knots the recursion references. None of the other six bases has such a
cutoff: every coefficient $c_g$ influences $\phi(x)$ at every $x$. For the
polynomial bases (Chebyshev, Lagrange, Newton) that influence does not decay
at all away from $z_g$, which is the algebraic reason they extrapolate worst
once the trajectory reaches states their coefficients were not fitted on
(Table~\ref{tab:basis-ablation}). The residual term
$w_b\,\mathrm{silu}(x)$ in Equation~\ref{eq:kan-edge} is the same for every
basis choice, since it is a fixed part of the edge parameterization, not one
of the substituted terms above.

B-spline edges out RBF on accuracy, at $3.9\times$ RBF's wall-clock time and
a higher Lipschitz estimate ($8.44$ vs.\ $6.91$). This result is for the
B-spline with clamped boundary knots (Section~\ref{sec:numerics}), which
keeps its basis functions summing to one right up to the edges of $[-1,1]$.
An earlier version used an unclamped knot vector, which left only about
$17\%$ coverage at the domain edges and produced a diverging model. A
tempting explanation of the higher Lipschitz estimate is that compact local
support lets the B-spline fit sharper local features. The longer-budget runs
do not support it: at 25{,}000 epochs the B-spline's estimate ($7.05$) is
\emph{lower} than RBF's at 50{,}000 ($7.75$, Table~\ref{tab:epoch-budget}),
so the ordering of $L$ between the two bases is not stable and is not
attributed to support here. The two bases are statistically tied on error
(6\% apart, inside single-seed noise), which makes RBF the better
accuracy-per-second choice.
Lagrange is the slowest basis (6829~s) even though its formula is closed
form, because this implementation builds each cardinal polynomial with an
unvectorized double loop over grid points: $G(G-1)$ small tensor operations
per basis call, on every layer of every field evaluation, so per-operation
overhead dominates. Its cost is an implementation artifact, not a property
of the basis.
Polynomial global-support bases (Chebyshev, Lagrange) fit the training
window well and extrapolate $4$--$5\times$ worse -- the expected signature
of a basis whose every coefficient influences the whole domain rather than a
local region of it. Support is not the whole story, however. RSWAF and IQF
are bell-shaped kernels just like the Gaussian, cost the same to evaluate,
and fit the training window comparably, yet they extrapolate $16\times$ (RSWAF)
and $11\times$ (IQF) worse than it. The shape of each kernel, not only whether it is
local, decides how the fitted field behaves outside the training window;
with one seed per basis, this gap is a measured ranking, not an explained
one. Newton's basis is the outlier on smoothness in the
opposite direction: its lowest Lipschitz constant in the table ($5.65$)
reflects underfitting, not desirable flatness, since it is also the only
basis that never reaches $10^{-3}$ training loss.

The training histories (Figure~\ref{fig:basis-curves}) show the same
ordering developing over time. The B-spline has the lowest loss early and
again from about epoch 6000 onward. Chebyshev is lowest in between and
reaches $10^{-3}$ first (at epoch 3892, against RBF's 5282), but it does not
keep that lead. Newton stays
an order of magnitude above every other basis throughout.

\subsection{Testing the paper's KAN-versus-MLP claims}

The base paper's two headline claims on Lotka-Volterra are that its 240-parameter
KAN-ODE is more accurate than a similarly sized MLP Neural ODE, and that it
gets there with about $10\times$ fewer epochs: $2.6\times10^{-5}$ training
loss after $10^4$ epochs, already below the MLP's $3\times10^{-5}$ after
$10^5$ \cite{koenig2024kanode}. Table~\ref{tab:kan-vs-mlp} tests both under
this project's protocol, against the paper's literal MLP and against a
parameter-matched MLP that trains.

At the 10{,}000-epoch budget the two claims split. On accuracy, KAN-ODE
extrapolates $5.4\times$ better than the parameter-matched MLP from
statistically identical training loss (7\% apart), which agrees with the
paper. On speed, the MLP reaches $10^{-3}$ training loss $4.1\times$
\emph{sooner} (epoch 1297 against 5282) and also reaches $10^{-4}$ first,
which contradicts the paper's tenfold claim. Per epoch, KAN-ODE costs
$2.3\times$ the paper-spec MLP ($0.1705$ vs.\ $0.0736$~s), close to the paper's
own ratio of $19$ against $7$ minutes per $10^4$ epochs
\cite{koenig2024kanode}; the deeper SiLU MLP is slower per epoch than both
because it has four sequential layers.

The accuracy advantage does not survive a longer run. Retrained to
50{,}000 epochs (last row; Section~\ref{sec:phase4}), the parameter-matched
MLP's extrapolation error falls to $5.90\times10^{-6}$, $6.8\times$
\emph{lower} than KAN-ODE's. Those reruns also used gradient clipping, which
the 10{,}000-epoch runs did not, and the two changes were not separated. The
KAN-ODE lead measured at 10{,}000 epochs is therefore a statement about that
setting, not a stable property of the two architectures on this problem.
Both runs use a single seed.

Figure~\ref{fig:kan-mlp-curves} shows where the speed difference comes from.
The SiLU MLP's training loss falls much faster over the first 1{,}000 epochs
and stays below the KAN-ODE's for most of the run, but it is interrupted by
large recurring spikes. The KAN-ODE descends more slowly and more smoothly,
and the two meet near $10^{-4}$ only at the end of the budget. The paper's
tanh MLP levels off near a training loss of $1$ by about epoch 2000 and does
not improve after that.

\paragraph{Phase-space geometry.} Trajectory error alone does not show how
each model fails. Figure~\ref{fig:phase-space-models} plots each model's
predicted orbit against the true limit cycle. The KAN-ODE and the SiLU MLP
both close the orbit correctly and are hard to tell apart by eye. Both show
slight jaggedness at the right-hand turning point, where the true orbit bends
most sharply, and the MLP's extrapolated segment sits slightly off the true
orbit near its top ($x\approx3$--$4$). The $5.4\times$ gap in extrapolation MSE
between them is therefore a small difference in absolute terms, not a
qualitative one. The paper-specification tanh MLP converges to a
different, stable but wrong closed orbit: its prey range is roughly right, but
its predator range collapses to $[1.0,1.9]$ against the true $[0.26,4.58]$, and
its training and extrapolated segments coincide, so it has not diverged. Its
Lipschitz estimate of $38.2$, against the KAN-ODE's $6.9$, reflects a much
sharper learned field. Figure~\ref{fig:lv-streamlines} shows the KAN-ODE's
learned vector field itself, whose streamlines carry the trajectory around
the orbit. Away from the orbit the field is not constrained by any data, and
its streamlines there need not match the true system's.

The conclusion this supports is narrow. Under this recipe, the paper's
$[2,50,2]$ tanh network fails to train, and Section~\ref{sec:phase4} confirms
that more epochs do not rescue it. It does not show that the paper's own MLP
result is wrong. The paper trained this network successfully, and its code
differs from this recipe in two ways that plausibly matter for a tanh
network: a \emph{higher} learning rate of $10^{-2}$ rather than
$2\times10^{-3}$, and initial weights divided by $10^5$, so the network starts
near zero (Table~\ref{tab:paper-audit}). Whether those settings rescue tanh
was not tested.

\subsection{Diagnosing and fixing two cross-domain failures}

Applying the Lotka-Volterra recipe unchanged to the damped pendulum and to
SIR failed on both systems, each for its own reason. Neither was fixed by a
hyperparameter search. The pendulum was settled by a small factorial control
that crossed training-window length with activation; SIR was diagnosed by
measuring the untrained network's field against the true one before any
training step.

\paragraph{Pendulum: extending the training window.} The pendulum recipe
originally trained on $t\in[0,3]$ and diverged in extrapolation
($R^2=-1.318$). Extending the training window to $t_{\text{train}}=5.0$ --
a single flag -- resolves it:

\begin{expectbox}
\expectrow{Question}{Why does the Lotka-Volterra recipe, applied unchanged, diverge on the pendulum?}
\expectrow{Expected (hypothesis)}{SiLU's residual branch cannot represent the sign-changing derivative $\dot\theta=\omega$ -- an architectural limit.}
\expectrow{Observed}{Refuted. The longer window removes the divergence under both activations, and SiLU extrapolates better of the two; SiLU fits the sign change fine once the window is long enough.}
\end{expectbox}

root-mean-square error of $0.0114$~rad/s and reproduces the true turning

\paragraph{SIR: a unit-scale mismatch.} The original 10{,}000-epoch SIR run
(gradient clipping at 1.0, no other fix) reached its best checkpoint at epoch
6925, saw its gradient norm climb to $5.82\times10^{18}$, produced its first
non-finite gradient at epoch 8686 and a NaN loss at epoch 8702, and ended
with invalid final weights.

\begin{expectbox}
\expectrow{Question}{What causes SIR's divergence, and is it a scale problem, an optimizer defect, or a modeling gap?}
\expectrow{Expected}{Measuring the untrained network's own field magnitude against the true dynamics, before any training, should reveal whether the two are mismatched in scale.}
\expectrow{Observed}{A $19\times$ speed mismatch at initialization. Rescaling time removes the divergence entirely; no optimizer or architecture change was needed.}
\end{expectbox}

Measuring the learned field magnitude
\emph{before any training step} explained the divergence directly. A
Glorot-initialised KAN starts with $|f_\theta|\approx0.203$ against SIR's
true field magnitude of $0.011$ -- a $19\times$ speed mismatch. To see why
this is catastrophic rather than merely inaccurate, consider that
backpropagation through an explicit solver differentiates roughly $T/\Delta t$
nested nonlinear steps, and the resulting gradient sensitivity grows
exponentially as $e^{\Ltrunc \Ttrain}$, where $\Ltrunc$ is the Lipschitz
constant of $\mathbf{f}_\theta$ and $\Ttrain$ is the integration horizon.
Lotka-Volterra trains at $\Ttrain=3.5$ with $|f|\sim10^0$ and converges;
the pendulum trains at $\Ttrain=5.0$ with $|f|\sim10^0$ and converges after
the window fix. SIR's true field is small ($|f|\sim10^{-2}$), but what sets
the sensitivity is the horizon: SIR is integrated to $\Ttrain=50$, and the
network's Lipschitz constant is $\Ltrunc=O(1)$ at initialization, so
$\Ltrunc\Ttrain\approx50$ and $e^{\Ltrunc\Ttrain}$ is astronomically large.
Trouble starts almost immediately: a
2{,}000-epoch diagnostic rerun of the same recipe shows the (pre-clipping)
gradient norm spiking to $2\times10^{6}$ at epoch 11. Clipping keeps each
update finite, so that run does not break down, but the spike shows the
sensitivity is present from the first epochs, not only near epoch 8686.

The optimizer was behaving rationally: with an epoch-0 loss of
$3.16\times10^{2}$, collapsing the field to $\mathbf{f}_\theta\to\mathbf{0}$
reduces the loss to $\approx10^{-1}$ -- a $3{,}000\times$ improvement
reachable immediately. SIR's natural timescale is the recovery period
$1/\gamma=10$~days; substituting $\tau=t/10$ gives
$\dd\mathbf{y}/\dd\tau = 10\,\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{y})$ over
$\tau\in[0,5]$, landing SIR in the same regime the other two systems already
train in. Projecting the learned field onto the zero-sum subspace
$f_{\text{proj}}(\mathbf{y}) = f(\mathbf{y})-\tfrac{1}{n}\sum_i f_i(\mathbf{y})$
(Equation~\ref{eq:projection}) fixes a separate mass-drift symptom by
enforcing the SIR invariant (Equation~\ref{eq:sir}) as a property of the flow
rather than a penalty on the fit.

These two fixes remove the divergence and the mass drift, but they leave the
end of the outbreak poorly predicted: extrapolation $R^2$ rises from
$-20.97$ only to $-0.145$ (Table~\ref{tab:sir-fix-summary}, middle column).
The remaining error comes from where the test window sits in state space.
The window $t\in(50,80]$ has $S\approx0.03$--$0.04$ and small $I$, a corner
the training data visits at only one of its 101 points. A third fix, the
vanishing-field gate on $I$ (Equation~\ref{eq:vanishing-gate}), supplies the
missing behaviour by construction: every SIR transition is proportional to
$I$, so the gated field goes to zero with $I$ exactly as the true one does.
With the gate added, extrapolation $R^2$ reaches $+0.9981$
(Figure~\ref{fig:sir-fixed}). Unlike the first
two fixes, the gate is a physical assumption about the system, so the two
results are reported separately.

\begin{keypoint}[Methodological lesson carried into later studies]
The SIR diagnosis came from measuring the untrained network's field against
the true field before any training step. That measurement showed that a
rescaling of time was needed before a single run was spent on it. It did not
set the factor: $s=10$ comes from placing the rescaled training horizon in
the regime where the other systems train ($T/s=5$), which for SIR coincides
with its recovery time $1/\gamma=10$ days.
The same init-time measurement is reused in the non-mechanistic epidemic test
of Section~\ref{sec:track-e}. The pendulum was settled differently, by a
factorial control, and is the reminder that a control arm is needed even when
a diagnosis looks mechanistic.
\end{keypoint}

\subsection{Pendulum energy dissipation: an independent physical check}

The fixed pendulum recipe captures $94.2\%$ of the true energy dissipation
and tracks $E(t)$ to $0.6\%$ inside the training window, but the strict
monotonic-decay invariant ($\dd E/\dd t\le0$, Equation~\ref{eq:pendulum})
is violated on 40 of 200 predicted steps. The model is under-damped:
the true angular displacement decays to $0.004$ by $t=9.1$ while the
predicted trajectory continues oscillating at $0.237$. This energy
diagnostic is an independent confirmation of the under-damping, expressed
as a violated conservation law rather than a trajectory RMSE
(Figure~\ref{fig:pendulum-energy}): after the training window ends at
$t=5$, the predicted energy stops tracking the true curve and stays well
above it, between about $0.5$ and $1.5$, with small upward jumps, while the
true energy keeps decaying toward zero.
