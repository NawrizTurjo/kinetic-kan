\section{Validation: Epoch Budget, Implementation Audit and Paper Claims}
\label{sec:phase4}

Every comparison so far was made at a fixed 10{,}000-epoch budget and under
this project's own recipe. This section tests both choices. It retrains the
configurations whose ranking matters most at up to five times the budget,
compares this project's recipe field by field with the base paper's text
and code, and sets each of the paper's claims next to what this project
measured.

\subsection{Epoch-budget sensitivity}

\begin{expectbox}
\expectrow{Question}{Are the rankings in Tables~\ref{tab:solver-ablation}--\ref{tab:kan-vs-mlp} a property of the configurations, or of stopping at 10{,}000 epochs?}
\expectrow{Expected}{Every configuration was still improving at 10{,}000 epochs, so absolute errors should fall; the ranking should hold if 10{,}000 epochs was long enough to separate them.}
\expectrow{Observed}{The ranking among KAN variants mostly holds. The KAN-versus-MLP comparison reverses: at 50{,}000 epochs (with gradient clipping) the parameter-matched MLP extrapolates $6.8\times$ better than KAN-ODE.}
\end{expectbox}

Five configurations were chosen to cover the comparisons a reader would
question first: Euler (the one solver that does not reach $10^{-4}$ training
loss within 10{,}000 epochs), Tsit5 with RBF (the reference point every other
entry is compared against), the cubic B-spline (statistically tied with RBF
in Table~\ref{tab:basis-ablation}), and both MLP baselines. Each was
retrained from scratch on cloud CPUs with the same architecture, data,
solver, learning rate and seed. Two things changed, not one. The obvious one
is the number of epochs. The other is gradient-norm clipping at $1.0$, which
the training code had adopted as its default after the first cross-domain
runs exploded (Sections~\ref{sec:numerics} and~\ref{sec:phase2}), whereas the
original 10{,}000-epoch Lotka-Volterra runs predate it and were unclipped. Clipping engaged on $4.3\%$ of steps for
Tsit5/RBF, $4.7\%$ for Euler, $2.2\%$ for the B-spline, $11\%$ for the SiLU
MLP and $99.3\%$ for the paper-specification MLP. For the first four it
rarely acts and should matter little, but the comparison below is not a pure
epoch-budget comparison, and the paper-specification MLP's extended run is
in effect a different optimizer. Four runs went to 50{,}000 epochs ($5\times$
the original budget). The B-spline ran to 25{,}000 ($2.5\times$), because an
early timing measurement projected a 50{,}000-epoch run beyond the length of
one compute session. That estimate later proved $1.6\times$ too pessimistic,
but the shorter budget was kept and every B-spline comparison below is
10{,}000 against 25{,}000 epochs.

Tables~\ref{tab:epoch-budget} and~\ref{tab:epoch-ranking} give three
separate answers.

\paragraph{The comparisons among KAN variants mostly hold.} At 10{,}000
epochs RBF and B-spline are $6\%$ apart in extrapolation error. The extended
runs are $6\%$ apart as well, but that compares B-spline at 25{,}000 epochs with
RBF at 50{,}000, so it is not a matched comparison. At a matched 25{,}000 epochs,
only the training loss can be compared exactly, because the RBF run saved a
checkpoint only at its best epoch overall. There, from the RBF run's own
training history, the B-spline is ahead: $9.65\times10^{-6}$ against
$1.28\times10^{-5}$, $25\%$ lower. So the two bases remain close, within a
factor of two at every budget checked, but ``statistically tied at every
budget'' is not established; with one seed, neither is a B-spline lead.
Euler stays last. Its gap to Tsit5 narrows from $28\times$ to
$4.8\times$, so part of Table~\ref{tab:solver-ablation}'s Euler penalty was
slower convergence rather than a permanent floor, but a first-order
integrator remains measurably worse after five times the training. These
checks cover the two ends of the solver table; the middle-order solvers were
not retrained.

\paragraph{The KAN-versus-MLP comparison reverses.} The parameter-matched
MLP improves $81\times$ in extrapolation error, against KAN-ODE's
$2.2\times$, and ends $6.8\times$ ahead on the near window and $10.8\times$
ahead on the far window (Table~\ref{tab:extrap-horizon-50k}). The KAN-ODE
advantage of Tables~\ref{tab:kan-vs-mlp} and~\ref{tab:extrap-horizon} is
therefore an advantage in how quickly it reaches a well-extrapolating field,
not in the best field it can reach on this problem. One confound remains:
clipping acted on $11\%$ of the MLP's steps but only $4.3\%$ of the KAN's,
and its effect was not separated from that of the longer budget. A 50{,}000-epoch
unclipped rerun of both would settle it.

\paragraph{The paper-specification MLP is not an epoch-budget problem.}
Given five times the budget, the $[2,50,2]$ tanh network does not converge:
its training loss barely moves ($1.08\to1.01$), its extrapolation gets worse
($R^2$ from $0.42$ to $-0.48$), its Lipschitz estimate nearly triples to
$104.8$, and gradient clipping engages on $99.3\%$ of steps (its median
unclipped gradient norm is $19.8$). More epochs, with clipping, do not rescue
it: its failure under this protocol comes from the recipe, not the budget.
The audit below identifies which parts of the recipe differ from the paper's.

\paragraph{Cost.} The five runs took $23.2$ hours of CPU time in total, with
$10.7$ hours of wall-clock time for the longest run. The cloud CPUs ran about
$2.3$--$2.5\times$ slower per epoch than the development machine used to plan
them, and a five-epoch timing sample overestimated the steady-state rate by
$1.6\times$ because it was dominated by start-up cost. Budget estimates for
runs like these should be calibrated on a few hundred epochs.

\subsection{Implementation audit against the base paper}
\label{sec:paper-audit}

The paper's Lotka-Volterra recipe was read from its text
\cite{koenig2024kanode} and from the driver script and layer code in its
official repository \cite{koenig2024kanoderepo}. The repository's own README
notes that its scripts were re-run shortly before release and ``are not the
exact runs reported in the manuscript'', so where text and code disagree,
both are listed. Table~\ref{tab:paper-audit} compares every setting that
could change a result.

Most settings match or differ for a reason whose effect this report
measures. Three do not: the RBF width, the initial-weight rescaling and the
learning rate. None was varied here, and the last two plausibly explain why
the paper's $[2,50,2]$ tanh MLP trains in the paper and fails here, since a
tanh network started at full Glorot scale and trained at a fifth of the
paper's MLP learning rate is a different optimization problem. Retraining
the paper-specification MLP with the paper's learning rate and
initialization is the direct test, and it is the most important open item
this audit leaves.

\subsection{The paper's claims against this project's measurements}

Table~\ref{tab:paper-claims} sets each quantitative Lotka-Volterra claim of
the base paper next to the corresponding measurement in this report. One
claim is reproduced outright: the 240-parameter KAN-ODE does learn the
dynamics from a single training cycle and extrapolates with $R^2$ above
$0.9999$. One is closely matched: KAN-ODE is a few times slower per epoch
than the paper's MLP, by $2.3\times$ here against the paper's $2.7\times$ (19
against 7 minutes per $10^4$ epochs), just below the paper's stated
$2.5$--$3\times$ range.

The paper's central efficiency claim, that KAN-ODE needs about $10\times$
fewer epochs than a similarly sized MLP, is not reproduced: under this
protocol the parameter-matched MLP reaches $10^{-3}$ training loss
$4.1\times$ \emph{sooner}. The paper's accuracy claim holds only at the
shorter budget. KAN-ODE extrapolates $5.4\times$ better at 10{,}000 epochs,
and $6.8\times$ worse at 50{,}000 (with the clipping caveat above). The
paper's own MLP result could not be reproduced at all, because its
$[2,50,2]$ tanh network does not train under this recipe. The audit
(Table~\ref{tab:paper-audit}) points to the learning rate and the
initial-weight scale as the likely reasons, so this row is a statement about
the recipe rather than evidence against the paper. The paper itself quotes
this MLP's final training loss inconsistently: as $3.0\times10^{-5}$ in the
text of its Section~III.1.1 and as $4.1\times10^{-5}$ in its Table~1 (width-50,
252-parameter row; arXiv version~3 \cite{koenig2024kanode}). Finally, the paper's interpretability
claim rests on $L_1$-regularized training and pruning, which this project did
not run, so it is neither confirmed nor refuted here.

\begin{keypoint}[Status]
The epoch-budget reruns are complete. The implementation audit is complete
as a comparison of settings, but none of the differences it found has been
tested by retraining. Together they show three things. The ordering among
KAN variants (Euler last, RBF and B-spline close) survives a longer budget.
The KAN-ODE's extrapolation advantage over a parameter-matched MLP holds at
10{,}000 epochs but not at 50{,}000, where gradient clipping also changed.
And three recipe differences from the paper (learning rate, initial-weight
scale and RBF width) remain unmeasured. Almost every result is still
single-seed.
\end{keypoint}
