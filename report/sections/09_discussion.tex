\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

Seven patterns recur across studies that were designed independently and
address unrelated questions. Their recurrence is itself informative about how
this class of model should be studied going forward.

\paragraph{A reasoned hypothesis still requires a control arm.} Three separate
studies advanced a specific, mechanistically plausible hypothesis and then
tested it directly: gradient-norm roughness as the cause of Euler's
convergence floor (Section~\ref{sec:track-b}), SiLU's representational limit
as the cause of the pendulum's early failure (Section~\ref{sec:phase2}), and
the predicted inapplicability of the vanishing-field gate to non-mechanistic
data (Section~\ref{sec:track-e}). Each hypothesis had a clear argument behind it
before any run was launched, and each was overturned by the run itself. The
result reported in every one of these three cases is the outcome of the
control arm, and the original argument survives only as the motivation for
running it. This is not a coincidence of three unlucky guesses; it reflects
the difficulty of reasoning about a system where the integrator, the
optimizer, and the learned function interact inside the same backpropagation
graph. The standard practice of ablating one variable while holding others
fixed rests on an assumption of near-independence that does not hold here.

\paragraph{A single seed and a single train/test split account for results
that first looked like properties of the solver or the method.} The
damping-sweep failures at $\mu\in\{0.5,1.0,2.0\}$ (Section~\ref{sec:track-d})
and the epidemic-fit extrapolation runaway (Section~\ref{sec:track-e}) both
appeared, on first inspection, to indict a solver or a modeling approach.
Retraining with additional seeds, in the damping-sweep case, and shifting the
train/test split by fifteen days, in the epidemic case, resolved the first
into seed-dependent local minima of training, apart from one Euler-specific
failure at $\mu=1.0$ ($\mu=0.1$ fails for every seed and solver, because its
trajectory never settles within the training window), and the runaway peak
(though not the decay tail) into an artifact of the window boundary. Neither
observation survives as a claim about solver order or about
KAN-ODE's capacity once the seed or the split is varied. Almost every
quantitative result in this report is $N=1$; the damping study's small extension
to $N=3$ was enough to dissolve a headline finding, which underscores how
much of the remaining project rests on the same single-seed foundation.

\paragraph{A fixed training budget is itself an experimental variable.}
Comparing models at one epoch count answers ``which is better after this
much training'', not ``which is better''. The two questions largely agreed
for the comparisons among KAN variants: with $2.5$--$5\times$ the budget, RBF
and B-spline stayed within a factor of two of each other and Euler stayed
last (Section~\ref{sec:phase4}). They disagreed for the comparison that
matters most to the base paper: the KAN-ODE's $5.3\times$ extrapolation lead
over a parameter-matched MLP at 10{,}000 epochs became a $6.8\times$ deficit
at 50{,}000. The longer runs also used gradient clipping, which the
10{,}000-epoch runs did not, and ran on a different platform (operating
system and PyTorch version), so that reversal is a combined effect of budget,
clipping and platform that this project did not separate. Rankings between models of
different families, which converge at different rates, need to be checked at
more than one budget, under otherwise identical settings, before they are
read as properties of the models.

\paragraph{Measuring a quantity before training is more informative than
tuning after a failed run.} The SIR divergence (Section~\ref{sec:phase2}), the
non-mechanistic epidemic fit (Section~\ref{sec:track-e}), and the damping sweep's
spurious equilibrium (Section~\ref{sec:track-d}) were each root-caused by
evaluating the model's own initial field, or its trained field at a candidate
equilibrium, and comparing that number against the system's true scale. In
every case this produced a specific, falsifiable prediction -- that time
must be rescaled, that a given fix should or should not transfer, where a
spurious equilibrium lies -- that a subsequent short run or a direct
computation confirmed or refuted, in contrast to the open-ended search a
hyperparameter sweep would have required. The measurement did not always
supply the numbers as well: the rescaling factors came from a separate
horizon rule, not from the measured mismatch (Section~\ref{sec:track-e}).

\paragraph{Training accuracy and generalization quality are decoupled.} On the
pendulum, the hybrid basis matches pure RBF's training loss to $1.7\%$ at 10{,}000 epochs
yet collapses in extrapolation ($R^2=-2.17$ vs.\ $+0.647$,
Section~\ref{sec:track-c}). The damping sweep's trapped models
(Section~\ref{sec:track-d}) reach training losses of $1.8\times10^{-2}$ --
qualitatively acceptable on a casual inspection -- while their learned field
is far from zero at the true equilibrium ($|\mathbf f_\theta(0,0)|\approx2.7$--$2.9$)
and has its fixed point elsewhere. And the rescaling-only epidemic model
(Section~\ref{sec:track-e}) produces a plausible training fit while predicting
an infected peak six times the physical maximum. In all three cases, a reader who judged the model solely by
training-window MSE would reach the wrong conclusion. The evaluation
protocol established in Section~\ref{sec:phase1} -- scoring strictly on
extrapolation, never on training loss (which is used only to select the
checkpoint) -- was adopted precisely because this
decoupling was anticipated, but these studies go further: they show that
configurations with numerically indistinguishable training fits can differ
by nearly an order of magnitude in extrapolation error (the pendulum hybrid
and pure RBF, $0.809$ against $0.090$, from training losses $1.7\%$ apart). The number to watch is always the one computed outside the training
window, and no training-window metric tested in this project reliably
predicts it.

\paragraph{On Lotka-Volterra, extra cost did not buy accuracy.} Across the
solver ablation (Section~\ref{sec:phase2}), the basis ablation, and the
hybrid-basis study (Section~\ref{sec:track-c}), the same pattern appears on
Lotka-Volterra at one seed. The cheapest methods that work (Midpoint at
$1/3$ the function evaluations of Tsit5; RBF at $1/3.9$ the wall-clock of
B-spline) match their more expensive alternatives about as closely as two
runs at one seed can be told apart; the $3.5\times$ gap between Midpoint and
Heun, two methods of equal cost, shows how large that single-seed spread can
be. Adding cost, whether more function evaluations per step, more basis
evaluations per edge, or a second basis evaluated in parallel, did not buy a
measurable accuracy improvement in these runs. The hybrid basis is the
clearest case, on both systems it was tried on: the blend pays roughly the
sum of both bases' per-epoch compute and never recovers that cost in
accuracy. Whether the pattern holds at more seeds, or on the other systems
for the solver and basis ablations, was not tested.

\paragraph{These KAN edges do not produce interpretable symbolic structure
without explicit encouragement.} The pruning experiment
(Section~\ref{sec:track-e}) found that zeroing the spline branches of the two weakest of forty edges
degrades a noise-free model to worse than a constant predictor -- a factor of
$3.9\times10^4$. The fit is spread across many edges, so no small set of
edges can be read off as the terms of the governing equation. This is
consistent with training without sparsity-inducing regularization, though
the regularized retraining that would confirm it was not run, and it means the interpretability
advantage that motivated the original choice of a smooth, globally-supported
basis function remains a hypothesis rather than a demonstrated property of
the trained models in this project. A future retraining with explicit
edge-level regularization would be needed to test whether sparse,
readable edge functions emerge when the training loss actively asks for them.
